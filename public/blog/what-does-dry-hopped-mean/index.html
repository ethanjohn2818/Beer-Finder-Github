What does 'dry-hopped' mean?

Published 11 July 2026 · by Katie

"Dry-hopped", "double dry-hopped", "DDH", these phrases are all over modern beer cans, always as a good thing. But what does dry-hopping actually mean, and why does it make a beer taste so fruity? It's simpler than it sounds.

Hops do two different jobs

To understand dry-hopping, you need to know that when you add hops changes what they do. Hops added to the boiling wort release bitterness but their delicate aromas boil away. Hops added after the boil, once the beer has cooled, keep all their aroma and flavour without adding bitterness. That second technique is dry-hopping.

Why it changed beer forever

Dry-hopping is the reason a modern hazy IPA can taste like tropical fruit juice with barely any harsh bitterness. By piling in aromatic hops after fermentation, sometimes several times over, brewers extract huge amounts of citrus, tropical and stone-fruit aroma. It's the single technique most responsible for the flavour of contemporary craft beer.

Double dry-hopped (DDH)

"Double dry-hopped" simply means an even bigger or a second dose of dry hops, for an extra-intense aroma. It's not an official measure, one brewer's DDH is another's standard, but it's a reliable signal that a beer will be loud and juicy.

The catch: freshness

All that fragile hop aroma fades fast, which is why dry-hopped beers are best drunk within weeks, not months. Check the date and drink them young. To learn which hops bring which flavours, read what hops actually taste like.
