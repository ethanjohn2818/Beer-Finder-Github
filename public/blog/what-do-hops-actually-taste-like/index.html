What do hops actually taste like?

Published 22 August 2026 · by Katie

Pick up almost any craft beer and the back of the can reads like a shopping list of names: Citra, Mosaic, Galaxy, Simcoe. These are hops — the flowers that give beer its bitterness and, more importantly for modern craft, its aroma and flavour. But unless you brew, those names don't tell you much. Here's what they actually taste like.

First, what is a hop doing in there?

Hops do two jobs. Added early in the boil they bring bitterness, which balances the sweetness of the malt. Added late — or after the boil, a technique called dry-hopping — they bring aroma and flavour without much extra bitterness. That second job is where all those fruity, juicy, tropical notes in a modern IPA come from. Same flower, very different result depending on when and how it's used.

The big-name hops, in plain English

  • Citra — exactly what it sounds like: grapefruit, lime and tropical fruit. The workhorse of the hazy-IPA world.
  • Mosaic — tropical fruit and blueberry with a hint of pine. Complex and a bit dank.
  • Galaxy — an Australian hop bursting with passionfruit and peach. Loud and juicy.
  • Simcoe — pine, citrus and a resinous, earthy edge. The classic "West Coast" character.
  • Amarillo — orange and floral, soft and easy-going.
  • Cascade — grapefruit and floral notes; the hop that basically started American craft beer.
  • Nelson Sauvin — a New Zealand oddball tasting of white wine and gooseberry. Unmistakable once you've had it.
  • East Kent Goldings & Fuggles — traditional English hops: earthy, herbal and gently floral. The backbone of a proper bitter.

Why two beers with the same hop taste different

Hops are only half the story. The malt, the yeast, the water and the brewer's choices all shift the final flavour. A West Coast IPA and a hazy IPA can use the very same Citra and taste worlds apart — one crisp and bitter, the other soft and juicy. So treat hop names as a strong hint, not a guarantee.
