Oktoberfestbier and Märzen: the beer in the stein, explained

Published 3 October 2026 · by Katie

When you picture Oktoberfest you probably picture the beer: a full litre of golden lager, glowing in a glass stein the size of a vase. But what exactly is that beer? It has a specific style, a protected name, and a history that runs back long before refrigeration. Here is what is actually in the Maß.

First, what "Oktoberfestbier" means

"Oktoberfestbier" is a protected term. Only the six Munich breweries allowed at the festival can call their beer by that name, and only beer brewed within the city of Munich, to the German purity law, qualifies. So it is not just a style, it is a designation of origin, a bit like Champagne. Everything poured in the tents is a strong, smooth Munich lager of around 6% ABV, noticeably stronger than an everyday Helles.

The purity law: just four ingredients

German brewing still nods to the Reinheitsgebot, the famous "purity law" dating from 1516, which originally allowed only water, barley and hops (yeast was added to the list once people understood what it did). Oktoberfest beer sticks to that clean, honest recipe. There is nothing to hide behind: the quality of the malt and the skill of the lagering is the whole show.

Märzen: brewed in March, drunk in autumn

The traditional Oktoberfest style is Märzen, which simply means "March beer". Before refrigeration, brewing in the warm summer months was difficult and risky, so Bavarian brewers brewed a stronger, maltier batch in March and stored it in cool caves packed with ice through the summer. By September the cellars needed emptying to make room for the new season's brewing, so what better moment to drink it all down at a festival.

Classic Märzen is amber to copper in colour, with a rich, bready, lightly toasted malt flavour, a touch of sweetness and a clean, dry finish. It is a lager, so it is crisp rather than heavy, but it has far more malt depth than a pale lager. If you like a beer with a bit of biscuit and caramel to it, this is your style.

The modern switch to golden Festbier

Here is the twist. The beer served at Oktoberfest today is mostly not amber Märzen any more. Since the 1990s the tents have moved to a paler, golden beer usually called Festbier or Wiesnbier. It is still a strong Munich lager at around 6%, but it is lighter in colour and a little more drinkable by the litre, which matters when you are ordering several. The deep amber Märzen lives on mainly in bottles, and especially in the versions exported abroad, where "Oktoberfest Märzen" remains the classic.

How it should taste

  • Festbier (modern): bright gold, soft and smooth, gentle bready malt, a clean bitter finish, deceptively easy to drink for its strength.
  • Märzen (traditional): amber, maltier and fuller, notes of toast, biscuit and light caramel, dry rather than sweet at the end.

Both are lagers brewed with patience rather than loud hops. If you want the wider picture of how lagers like these are made, our guide to craft lager and pilsner is a good next read.

Where to find it in the UK

True imported Oktoberfestbier comes and goes seasonally, but Munich Helles, Weissbier and other Bavarian lagers from the festival breweries are around all year.
