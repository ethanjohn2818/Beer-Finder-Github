What is Oktoberfest? The world's biggest beer festival explained

Published 4 October 2026 · by Katie

Oktoberfest is the biggest beer festival on earth. Every autumn around six million people pour into Munich, the capital of Bavaria in southern Germany, to sit in enormous tents, eat roast chicken and pretzels, and drink litre after litre of specially brewed lager. It runs for about sixteen days, and despite the name most of it happens in September, finishing on the first weekend of October.

If you have ever wondered why it is called Oktoberfest when it starts in September, where the beer comes from, or what the fuss is about, here is the whole story in plain English.

It started with a royal wedding

The first Oktoberfest was not a beer festival at all. It was a wedding party. On 12 October 1810, Crown Prince Ludwig of Bavaria (later King Ludwig I) married Princess Therese of Saxe-Hildburghausen. The citizens of Munich were invited to celebrate on the fields just outside the city gates, with food, music and a horse race to finish.

Those fields were named Theresienwiese, meaning "Therese's meadow", in the new princess's honour. Locals still call the festival grounds "die Wiesn" to this day. The party was such a success that they held it again the next year, and the year after, and a tradition was born. Over two centuries it grew from a horse race into the beer-soaked spectacle we know now.

Why September, not October?

The early festivals really were held in October. Over the years the start was gradually pulled forward into September, for the simple reason that the weather is warmer and the evenings lighter. Sitting in a beer garden is a lot nicer in mild September than in cold, wet late October. The festival still stretches into the first days of October, so the name is not entirely wrong, it just leans early.

The opening: "O'zapft is!"

Oktoberfest officially begins at noon on the first Saturday, when the Mayor of Munich taps the first barrel of beer in the Schottenhamel tent. He hammers a tap into the keg, and once the beer flows he cries "O'zapft is!", Bavarian dialect for "It's tapped!". A twelve-gun salute follows, and only then is any brewery allowed to start serving. It is a proper ceremony, and getting the keg tapped cleanly in as few blows as possible is a point of mayoral pride.

Only six breweries are allowed

This is the part most people do not know. The beer served at Oktoberfest can only come from six breweries, all of them based inside Munich's city limits: Augustiner, Hacker-Pschorr, Hofbräu, Löwenbräu, Paulaner and Spaten. Their festival beer is brewed to a protected standard, and the style has its own story, which we cover in our guide to Oktoberfestbier and Märzen.

More than just beer

For all the litre steins, Oktoberfest is also a folk festival. There are grand parades on the opening weekend, funfair rides, brass bands playing oompah, and a sea of people in traditional Bavarian dress: Lederhosen for the men, Dirndl dresses for the women. We walk through the tents, the food and the traditions in our guide to what actually happens inside the Wiesn.

Can't get to Munich?

You do not need a flight to raise a stein. A handful of proper Bavarian beers from Oktoberfest breweries, and others brewed in the same spirit, turn up on UK supermarket shelves.
