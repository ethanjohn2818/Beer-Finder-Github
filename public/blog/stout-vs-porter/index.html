Stout vs porter: what's the actual difference?

Published 25 August 2026 · by Katie

Two dark beers sit side by side on the shelf: one calls itself a porter, the other a stout. They look almost identical in the glass. So what's the difference, and does it even matter? Here's the honest version.

A shared history

Porter came first, in 18th-century London. "Stout" originally just meant a stouter, that is, stronger, porter. So a stout was literally a bigger porter. Over centuries the two drifted apart, but that shared root is why the line between them is still blurry, and why brewers don't always agree on which name to use.

How they tend to taste

As a rough guide: porters lean towards chocolate, caramel and a rounder, sweeter, sometimes nutty character. Stouts lean towards roasted, coffee-like bitterness and a drier finish, thanks to roasted barley. Porters often feel a little softer and sweeter; stouts a little darker and more bitter. But there's huge overlap, and plenty of exceptions.

The many kinds of stout

Stout has splintered into a whole family. Dry (Irish) stout like Guinness is light-bodied and roasty. Milk stout adds lactose for a sweeter, creamier pint. Oatmeal stout uses oats for a silky texture. Imperial stout is the big, boozy, dessert-like end of the scale. Porters vary less, but you'll still find smoky, Baltic and pastry versions.

Does the label really matter?

Honestly, not much. The name is a starting hint, not a rule, a strong chocolatey "porter" and a smooth "stout" can taste nearly the same. Better to read the description on the can: words like "roasty", "coffee" and "dry" point one way; "chocolate", "caramel" and "smooth" point another.

Where to start

If you're new to dark beer, a milk or oatmeal stout is the friendliest way in, smooth, sweet and easy. From there, try a dry stout and a chocolate porter back to back and see which you prefer.
