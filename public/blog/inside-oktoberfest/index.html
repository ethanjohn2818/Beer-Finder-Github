Inside Oktoberfest: tents, steins and Bavarian traditions

Published 2 October 2026 · by Katie

Oktoberfest is famous for its beer, but turn up expecting a drinks event and you will be surprised. It is a full-blown folk festival, with funfair rides, brass bands, parades and more roast chicken than you can imagine, all wrapped around fourteen gigantic beer tents. Here is what it is actually like inside the Wiesn.

The tents are the whole festival

These are not little gazebos. The big Oktoberfest tents are temporary timber halls that seat several thousand people each, and in peak hours they fill completely. Every tent is run by one of the six Munich breweries and has its own character:

  • Schottenhamel is where the Mayor taps the first keg to open the festival, a young person's favourite.
  • Hofbräu-Festzelt is the loudest and most international, popular with visitors from around the world.
  • Hacker-Festzelt is nicknamed "Himmel der Bayern", the Heaven of the Bavarians, for its painted sky ceiling.
  • Augustiner-Festhalle is the local favourite, calmer and family-friendly, with beer still served from wooden barrels.
  • Schützen-Festzelt and the ox-roasting Ochsenbraterei round out the classics.

A tip locals will tell you: the tents are free to enter, but on busy evenings and weekends they shut the doors once full. Go in the morning or midweek if you want a table without booking.

The litre stein

Beer at Oktoberfest is served in one unit only: the Maß, a full litre in a heavy glass stein. There are no halves. You also cannot simply walk to a bar, you order from the waiters and waitresses, who carry astonishing numbers of full steins at once and expect a tip. The beer is strong, around 6%, so a couple of these is plenty for most people. Pace yourself, and never try to leave with the glass, the steins are counted.

"Ein Prosit" and the art of the toast

Every so often the band strikes up "Ein Prosit der Gemütlichkeit", a short drinking song that roughly toasts good cheer and cosiness. The whole tent stands, raises their steins, sings along and finishes with a loud "Prost!" before drinking. Gemütlichkeit, that warm feeling of good company, is really the point of the entire festival. When you clink steins, look the other person in the eye, it is considered bad luck not to.

What to eat

The food is half the fun and soaks up the beer nicely:

  • Hendl, half a roast chicken, the single most ordered dish at the festival.
  • Schweinshaxe, a huge roasted pork knuckle with crackling.
  • Brezn, soft pretzels the size of a dinner plate.
  • Weißwurst, pale veal sausages traditionally eaten before noon with sweet mustard.
  • Obatzda, a spiced soft-cheese spread, and Steckerlfisch, whole fish grilled on a stick.

Lederhosen, Dirndl and the famous bow

Almost everyone, locals and tourists alike, dresses up in traditional Bavarian Tracht. Men wear Lederhosen, leather breeches with braces, and women wear the Dirndl, a fitted dress with a blouse and apron. There is even a quiet code in how a woman ties her apron bow: on the left usually means single, on the right means spoken for. It is taken lightly, but it is real.

Bring the Wiesn home

You can capture a good slice of all this without leaving the UK: the right beer, a pretzel, some sausages and a loud playlist go a long way. See our guide to celebrating Oktoberfest at home, and pick your beer from the Oktoberfest beers page. Prost!
